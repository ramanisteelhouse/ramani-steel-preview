Metallurgy glossary

The terms that decide which grade you order — defined plainly, and linked to the grades where each one matters.

These are general definitions of standard metallurgical practice. They describe what the terms mean, not what we hold in stock. Where a term decides a grade choice, the relevant grade pages are linked beneath it.

PREN

Pitting resistance equivalent number

PREN is a single number that ranks how well a stainless steel resists pitting and crevice corrosion in chlorides, calculated as %Cr + 3.3 × %Mo + 16 × %N. Typical values are around 25 for 316L, about 35 for duplex 2205 and above 40 for super duplex grades. It compares alloys; it does not predict service life.

Where it matters: Duplex 2205 (S31803), Super Duplex 2507 (S32750), Super Duplex S32760, Stainless Steel 316L

Sensitisation

Sensitisation is the loss of corrosion resistance that happens when chromium carbides form at the grain boundaries of a stainless steel held between roughly 425 and 870 °C. The chromium locked into those carbides is no longer available to protect the metal beside them, so the boundaries corrode preferentially. Welding is the usual cause.

Where it matters: Stainless Steel 304L, Stainless Steel 316L, Stainless Steel 321H

Knife-line attack

Knife-line attack is intergranular corrosion in a narrow band immediately beside a weld in a stabilised stainless steel. It occurs when a later high-temperature exposure dissolves the stabilising carbides and carbon re-precipitates as chromium carbide on cooling. Titanium-stabilised 321H is more prone to it than niobium-stabilised 347H.

Where it matters: Stainless Steel 321H, Stainless Steel 347H

Stabilised stainless steel

A stabilised stainless steel contains titanium or niobium deliberately added to combine with carbon before chromium can, which prevents sensitisation. Grade 321 is stabilised with titanium and 347 with niobium. Both are specified where the steel will be welded and then serve at temperature.

Where it matters: Stainless Steel 321H, Stainless Steel 347H

H grade

Controlled carbon grade

An H suffix means the grade's carbon is held to a controlled range — typically 0.04 to 0.10 percent — rather than only to a maximum. The higher carbon gives better strength and creep resistance at elevated temperature. It is a chemistry restriction, not a heat treatment, so 304H and 304 differ in composition rather than condition.

Where it matters: Stainless Steel 304H, Stainless Steel 321H, Stainless Steel 347H, Incoloy 800H

ELI

Extra low interstitial

ELI means the interstitial elements — oxygen, nitrogen, carbon and iron — are held to tighter limits than in the standard version of the same alloy. The result is slightly lower strength but markedly better fracture toughness and ductility, including at cryogenic temperature. Titanium Grade 23 is the ELI version of Grade 5.

Where it matters: Titanium Grade 23 (Ti-6Al-4V ELI), Titanium Grade 5 (Ti-6Al-4V)

Annealed

Annealed describes metal that has been heated and cooled to soften it, relieve internal stress and restore a uniform structure. It is the condition most mill products are supplied in, and the condition the minimum properties in most ASTM tables refer to. Solution annealing is the equivalent treatment for austenitic and nickel alloys.

Solution annealed

Solution annealing heats an alloy until its alloying elements dissolve into a single phase, then cools it fast enough to keep them there. In austenitic stainless and nickel alloys it dissolves the carbides that would otherwise sit at grain boundaries, which is what restores corrosion resistance after welding or hot working.

Where it matters: Hastelloy C276, Inconel 625, Incoloy 800H

Precipitation hardening

Age hardeningH900H1150

Precipitation hardening strengthens an alloy by forming fine particles within the metal during a controlled ageing step. Condition names such as H900 and H1150 give the ageing temperature in °F: H900 is the strongest and least ductile, H1150 the toughest and weakest. Strength depends so heavily on the condition that it must be stated on the order.

Where it matters: 17-4PH (SUS 630), 15-5PH, Inconel 718, Monel K-500

Mill test certificate

MTCMaterial test certificate

A mill test certificate is the document in which the producing mill reports the actual chemical composition and mechanical test results for a specific heat of material, identified by its heat number. It is what a buyer checks against the standard's limits. The limits published in a standard describe what is permitted, never what a particular heat contains.

Heat number

Cast number

A heat number identifies a single batch of metal melted together, and is the link between a physical piece of material and its mill test certificate. It is marked on the material and quoted on the paperwork, which is what makes traceability possible.

UNS number

Unified Numbering System

A UNS number is a six-character designation identifying a metal's composition unambiguously across standards and countries — S31600 for stainless 316, N06625 for Inconel 625, R50400 for titanium Grade 2. The opening letter indicates the family. It identifies composition only, and says nothing about form, condition or properties.

Werkstoff number

W. Nr.DIN material number

A Werkstoff number is the German material designation used across European standards, written as a digit, a full stop and four more digits — 1.4404 for stainless 316L, 2.4856 for Inconel 625. European drawings and enquiries often quote it instead of a UNS number or a trade name.

Duplex stainless steel

Duplex stainless steel has a structure of roughly equal parts austenite and ferrite, which gives it about twice the yield strength of a standard austenitic grade along with better resistance to chloride pitting and stress corrosion cracking. Super duplex grades carry higher chromium, molybdenum and nitrogen, for a PREN above 40.

Where it matters: Duplex 2205 (S31803), Super Duplex 2507 (S32750), Super Duplex S32760

Chloride stress corrosion cracking

Chloride SCC

Chloride stress corrosion cracking is the sudden cracking of a stainless steel under the combination of tensile stress, chlorides and temperature, usually above about 60 °C. Standard austenitic grades such as 304 and 316 are vulnerable; duplex, super duplex and high-nickel alloys resist it far better. It gives little warning, because the metal loses almost no thickness first.

Where it matters: Duplex 2205 (S31803), Super Duplex 2507 (S32750), Incoloy 825

Sour service

NACE MR0175ISO 15156

Sour service means exposure to hydrogen sulphide, which can crack susceptible metals under stress. NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156 governs material selection for it, listing each corrosion-resistant alloy with the limits it is qualified within — H₂S partial pressure, temperature, chloride content and pH. An alloy is never simply approved; it is qualified for stated conditions.

Where it matters: Incoloy 825, Inconel 625, Duplex 2205 (S31803), Super Duplex 2507 (S32750)

Passive film

Passivation

The passive film is the thin, self-repairing chromium oxide layer that makes stainless steel stainless. It reforms on its own where oxygen is present, which is why a scratch does not start rust. Chlorides break it down locally, which is where pitting begins, and molybdenum makes it more resistant to that attack.

Theoretical weight

Theoretical weight is the weight of a piece of material calculated from its nominal dimensions and the density of the alloy. It is arithmetic, used for estimating freight and cost. Actual weight varies within the dimensional tolerance of the product, so it is never a substitute for weighing what was supplied.
